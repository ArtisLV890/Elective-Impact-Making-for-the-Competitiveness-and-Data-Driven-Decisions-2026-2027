# Knowledge districts worldwide: one anchor university, or several similar ones?

Research note, 9 November 2026.

**Question:** The Riga Knowledge Mile has five universities that largely offer the same things. Is that seen anywhere else, or are knowledge districts usually built around one university and the infrastructure around it?

## Short answer

1. **Most knowledge districts have one dominant anchor.** Brookings calls this the "anchor plus" model (Katz & Wagner, 2014). Kendall Square is built around MIT, Otaniemi around Aalto, and Eindhoven around TU/e.
2. **Districts with several universities do exist, but the universities almost always have different profiles.** One is research-intensive and the other applied, or one does medicine and another engineering. Examples are Pittsburgh, Manchester, Montreal, Melbourne and Sydney.
3. **Where similar institutions sit next to each other, two responses recur:**
   - a **consortium** that shares services and lets students take each other's courses (Colleges of the Fenway; Longwood in Boston);
   - a **merger** (Strasbourg, Grenoble, Aalto, Manchester; in Latvia, Banku augstskola into LU in 2025).
4. **Theory predicts the Riga problem.** If partners are too similar (high cognitive proximity), they have little to learn from each other and compete for the same students and funding. Collaboration needs partners who are related but different (Boschma, 2005; Frenken et al., 2007).

## 1. The typical model: one anchor

Katz & Wagner (2014) describe three types of innovation district:
- **"Anchor plus":** mixed-use development around major anchor institutions. The standard example is Kendall Square (MIT).
- **"Re-imagined urban areas":** former industrial or waterfront land.
- **"Urbanised science parks":** suburban research parks made denser.

Most famous districts are "anchor plus" with **one** university at the centre. Infrastructure, firms and start-ups cluster around it.

## 2. Districts with several universities: profiles usually differ

| District | Universities | How they differ |
|---|---|---|
| **Pittsburgh, Oakland** | University of Pittsburgh, Carnegie Mellon, UPMC (hospital system) | Pitt for medicine and life sciences, CMU for computing and robotics, UPMC for health care. Brookings (2017): the district needs to be "defined, marketed, and better connected". InnovatePGH now coordinates it. |
| **Manchester, Oxford Road Corridor** | University of Manchester, Manchester Metropolitan, NHS trust | Research-intensive versus applied/teaching university, plus hospitals. A **partnership board** with the city council and private partners (Bruntwood); MMU's vice-chancellor chairs it. Has a 10-year strategy (Vision 2030). |
| **Montreal, Quartier de l'innovation** | ÉTS and McGill (founders, 2013), then Concordia and UQAM | ÉTS is an engineering school; McGill is a research university. McGill described the partnership as building on **complementary strengths**. Supported by the city, Quebec, Canada and about 30 private partners. |
| **Melbourne Innovation Districts** | University of Melbourne, RMIT | Research university versus technical and design university. A three-way partnership with the City of Melbourne. Commentators say it has not yet gained the traction its potential calls for. |
| **Sydney, Tech Central** | University of Technology Sydney, University of Sydney (and others) | Technical versus comprehensive research university. "Digital Central" MOU (2023) for joint digital research. Has a state government strategy. |

**Pattern:** even in these districts, programmes overlap in mass subjects such as business. But the universities **position themselves differently**, and the district is built around the complementary parts.

## 3. Where similar institutions sit side by side: consortium or merger

**Consortium: Colleges of the Fenway (Boston)**
- Five neighbouring colleges with overlapping undergraduate offers.
- Full-time students can take **up to two courses per semester** at the other colleges, if seats are available. The courses count as "in-residence" credits.
- About **300–400 students cross-register each semester**.
- This is the closest real-world model for a Knowledge Mile Study Agreement. It shows that similar institutions can cooperate when the shared benefit is choice and services, not research complementarity.

**Shared organisation: Longwood Medical and Academic Area (Boston)**
- MASCO (now the Longwood Collective) was set up in 1972 by the area's hospitals and academic institutions.
- They pledged to work together on land use, parking, traffic and **"duplication of services"**.
- It is an early example of neighbouring institutions creating a joint body because of overlap.

**Mergers**
- Strasbourg (2009) and Grenoble Alpes reunited universities that had been split into three in the 1970s.
- Aalto (2010) merged three *complementary* schools (technology, business, art and design) to create new combinations.
- Paris-Saclay combined more than a dozen institutions, partly to gain visibility in rankings.
- Manchester merged with UMIST in 2004.
- Commentators warn that mergers can create "cumbersome" management structures (Times Higher Education).

**Latvia already debates this**
- The Ministry of Finance has said Latvia has excessive duplication of study programmes relative to the number of students.
- FICIL position papers (2017, 2019) criticise fragmentation and duplicated programmes.
- LU planned to consolidate about 30 programmes.
- Banku augstskola became LU's business school in 2025, under a consolidation plan the Cabinet approved in May 2023.

## 4. Theory: why duplication blocks collaboration

- **Boschma (2005):** too much cognitive proximity causes lock-in, so there is little new to learn. Partners need some cognitive distance.
- **Nooteboom (2000):** there is an optimal cognitive distance, close enough to understand each other and different enough to learn something new. Broekel & Boschma (2012) find empirical support for such an optimum.
- **Frenken, Van Oort & Verburg (2007):** regions grow more from **related variety**, sectors that are different but connected, than from many copies of the same thing.
- **Implication (our own reasoning, not a finding of these papers):** five universities teaching the same programmes are mainly **competitors** for the same students and state-funded places. That gives them little reason to cooperate, which helps explain why the 2024 memorandum stalled.

## 5. What this means for the Riga case

1. **Check the premise.**
   - Overlap is strongest in teaching of business and management (LU, RTU, RISEBA, Turība), law (LU, RSU, Turība) and communication.
   - The **research cores differ**: RTU in engineering and IT, RSU in medicine and health, LU in natural sciences and the humanities, RISEBA and Turība in business, design and media.
   - So the five are not "exactly the same". They are similar in teaching and different in research.
2. **Reframe the Mile around complementarities**, as Pittsburgh, Montreal and Aalto do. Examples are medtech (RTU + RSU), health economics and management (RSU + LU + the business schools) and creative tech (RISEBA + RTU).
3. **Use the overlap for the student side.** The Fenway model shows that similar institutions can still offer students more choice through cross-registration. This supports our Study Agreement proposal.
4. **Be ready for the question "why not merge?"** The government already pushes consolidation. Our answer is that a coordinator and a study agreement are a cheaper, reversible first step. The district can later agree who specialises in what (a profiling agreement), without the costs of a full merger.

## Possible next steps

- Count overlapping programmes by field for the five universities, using the AIC study programme register. This would turn "pretty much the same" into a number.
- Check Glasgow (three universities in one city) and Leiden–Delft–Erasmus (a complementary alliance) as further comparisons.

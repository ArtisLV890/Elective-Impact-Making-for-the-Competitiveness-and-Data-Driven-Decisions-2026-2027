# Bottom line

- **The evidence is strong on knowledge and modest on behaviour.**
  - Across 76 randomised trials, financial education raises knowledge by about **0.2 SD** and behaviour by about **0.1 SD**.
  - In school programmes, knowledge rises by **0.15–0.33 SD** and behaviour by about **0.07 SD**.
  - These are normal sizes for an education intervention, not miracle numbers. Say that openly; it builds credibility.
- **The best school programmes look like what we propose:**
  - they sit inside existing courses;
  - they come with ready-made materials and trained teachers;
  - they reach parents as a side effect.
- **Peru is the closest model to Latvia.** Financial education *replaced the economics hours* in the existing history, geography and economics course. Students gained knowledge, teachers gained even more, and years later the students who borrowed were behind on fewer loans. All this for about **US$5–7 per student**.
- **Two warnings from the evidence:**
  - Teaching about credit can briefly *increase* risky borrowing (Brazil). So the module must cover the costs of credit, not just access to it.
  - A mandate on paper does nothing (US, Cole et al.). That is Latvia's situation today.

# 1. Meta-analyses: how big are the effects?

## Kaiser & Menkhoff (2020): school programmes only

- **Paper:** "Financial education in schools: A meta-analysis of experimental studies", *Economics of Education Review* 78.
- **Data:** 37 experimental and quasi-experimental school studies.
- **Effects:**
  - financial **knowledge +0.33 SD**;
  - financial **behaviour +0.07 SD**;
  - in the 18 randomised studies only: **knowledge +0.15 SD**, behaviour +0.07 SD.
- **Intensity:** more intensive programmes (more hours) work better, but each extra hour adds less ("decreasing marginal returns"). The earlier working paper also links smaller classes to better results.
- **What it means for us:**
  - A dedicated block of about 10–12 lessons is justified.
  - We do not need a whole new subject.
  - Scattered single mentions are too little.
- **Say it as:** "+0.15 to +0.33 SD on knowledge". Quote the randomised figure (0.15) as the conservative one.

## Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban (2022): all financial education

- **Paper:** "Financial education affects financial knowledge and downstream behaviors", *Journal of Financial Economics* 145(2).
- **Data:** 76 randomised trials, over 160,000 people, 33 countries on six continents.
- **Effects:**
  - knowledge about **+0.2 SD**;
  - behaviour about **+0.1 SD** (about 0.09 unweighted);
  - the effect holds across **saving, budgeting, credit, insurance and retirement planning**.
- **Context:** the authors call these sizes "similar to those of educational interventions" in other subjects. The effects survive correction for publication bias.
- **Compared with older work:** the effects are 3–5 times larger than in the first sceptical meta-analysis (Fernandes et al. 2014). That study included far fewer randomised trials and many weak programmes.
- **Decay and timing:** effects shrink over time. Programmes tied to a real "teachable moment" do better.
- **What it means for us:**
  - Education works on average. The sceptics' "it doesn't work" view is outdated.
  - Effects fade, so we need repetition: grade 9, grade 11, and practice through the year.

# 2. Large school trials: what worked in practice

## Bruhn et al. (2016): Brazil

- **Paper:** "The impact of high school financial education: Evidence from a large-scale evaluation in Brazil", *AEJ: Applied Economics* 8(4).
- **Design:**
  - a randomised trial in **892 schools, about 25,000 students**, in 6 states;
  - financial education was **built into existing subjects** using ready-made case-study materials, with teacher training.
- **Short-term results:**
  - financial proficiency **+0.25 SD**;
  - more saving (+1.4 pp saving for purchases) and more budgeting;
  - school pass rates rose;
  - **parents also improved** ("trickle-up") in knowledge, saving and spending.
- **The cautionary result:** students also used **more expensive consumer credit** in the short run, and fell behind on more payments.
- **Long-term follow-up (up to 9 years later, Brazilian central bank working paper 563):**
  - the credit effect **reversed**: treated students were **less** likely to use the most expensive credit, and less likely to have overdue loans.
- **What it means for us:**
  - Integration into existing subjects can work at national scale.
  - Teaching about credit must stress its costs.
  - Measure the effect over years, not months.

## Frisancho (2023): Peru, the closest model to Latvia

- **Paper:** "Is school-based financial education effective? Immediate and long-lasting impacts on high school students", *Economic Journal* 133(651), 1147–1180.
- **Correction:** our slide cited this as "Frisancho (2020), IDB". Frisancho's 2020 paper in the *Economics of Education Review* is a *review* of youth financial education. The Peru trial is the 2023 *Economic Journal* paper; the IDB working paper is an earlier version.
- **Design:**
  - **300 public secondary schools** (150 matched pairs), grades 9–11, the national programme *Finanzas en mi Colegio*;
  - schools received **workbooks, teacher training and teacher guides**;
  - **financial education replaced the economics hours** in the existing history, geography and economics course.
- **Results:**
  - **Students:** financial literacy **+0.14 to +0.16 SD** (sources differ slightly), and more financial autonomy and financial savviness. Studies citing it report no harm to other school results.
  - **Teachers:** financial literacy **+0.32 SD**, more than twice the student effect. Training teachers improves the teachers too.
  - **Credit (credit-bureau data):** no change in *whether* students borrowed. But among students who had loans, **arrears fell by about 20%**.
  - **Long-term follow-up (Frisancho et al. 2025):** about 60,000 people, **seven years later**, public credit bureau. Treated students with loans had **about 20% less delinquent debt**.
  - **Parents** (Journal of Financial Literacy and Wellbeing, 2023): about 26% lower default probability and about 5% higher credit scores, strongest in poorer households.
  - **Cost:** about **US$5–7 per student** (GFLEC overview: about $5; our earlier notes: $6.6).
- **What it means for us:**
  - It shows our model works: replace part of the existing course's economics content, give ready-made materials, train the teachers.
  - The benefits reach students, teachers and parents, at low cost.

## Cannistrà et al. (2024): an online game in Europe

- **Paper:** Cannistrà, De Beckker, Agasisti, Amagir, Põder, Vartiak & De Witte, "The impact of an online game-based financial education course: Multi-country experimental evidence", *Journal of Comparative Economics* 52(4), 825–847.
- **Design:**
  - a randomised trial of an online game-based course (an escape-room style game) in several European countries;
  - co-author Põder is from Estonia, and Estonia is among the countries.
- **Results:**
  - financial literacy **+0.31 SD** in the short term;
  - the effect **varies a lot between countries**.
- **Note:** a citing study warns that cheap digital tools can produce smaller gains than a full course. So games work as reinforcement, not as a replacement for lessons.
- **What it means for us:**
  - This supports our gamified practice (escape rooms, the Olympiad).
  - It is European evidence, including Estonia.
- **Still to check in the paper:** the exact list of countries and the number of students. Our earlier notes say 2,220 students.

# 3. Implementation: teachers and mandates

## Compen, De Witte & Schelfhout (2019): teachers

- **Paper:** "The role of teacher professional development in financial literacy education: A systematic literature review", *Educational Research Review* 26, 16–31.
- **Finding:** teacher professional development is a key and **under-studied** ingredient of financial education.
- **What makes teacher training effective:**
  - a focus on content;
  - active learning;
  - enough duration (secondary sources cite **at least about 14 hours** for lasting effects);
  - coherence with the curriculum;
  - collaboration.
- **Supporting evidence:** Peru (teachers +0.32 SD), and a later Belgian study that trained teachers through an online module and improved *students'* literacy (*Education Economics*, 2023).
- **What it means for us:** our teacher-training element should be about 14+ hours, content-focused and practical. It fits inside the 36 hours every 3 years that teachers already need (Cabinet Regulation No. 569).

## Cole, Paulson & Shastry (2016): a mandate alone fails

- **Paper:** "High school curriculum and financial outcomes: The impact of mandated personal finance and mathematics courses", *Journal of Human Resources* 51(3), 656–698.
- **Design:** US state graduation requirements, compared over time and across states. Data from the census, credit records (New York Fed Consumer Credit Panel) and the SIPP household survey.
- **Results:**
  - **Personal finance mandates had no effect** on investment income, assets, home equity or credit outcomes.
  - **Extra mathematics courses did help:** more investing and investment income, more assets, **fewer credit card delinquencies and foreclosures**.
- **Why the mandates failed:** these were older mandates, often without dedicated courses, standards, tests or trained teachers. In other words, "compulsory on paper".
- **What it means for us:**
  - This is Latvia today.
  - Maths and reading skills matter too, so PISA 2025's decline is a warning.
  - The fix is real teaching, not another mandate.

## Urban, Schmeiser, Collins & Brown (2020): mandates that work

- **Paper:** "The effects of high school personal financial education policies on financial behavior", *Economics of Education Review* 78.
- **Design:**
  - **Georgia, Idaho and Texas** introduced personal finance requirements around 2007, with standards and course content;
  - students were compared before and after, and against neighbouring states without requirements, using credit-bureau data.
- **Results:**
  - young adults schooled after the requirement had **higher credit scores and lower delinquency**;
  - the effect **grew in later years** after the policy started;
  - in Georgia, 30-day delinquency fell by about **1.8 percentage points**;
  - Texas showed similar gains; Idaho's were smaller.
- **What it means for us:**
  - Cole et al. versus Urban et al. is the key contrast: **the same kind of mandate fails on paper and works with real course content**.
  - It also shows why results appear only after several years.

# 4. Lessons for our strategy

| Evidence | Lesson | In our proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020 | Intensity matters, with decreasing returns | About 10–12 lessons in grades 9 and 11, not a new subject |
| Kaiser et al. 2022 | Effects are real but fade | Repetition, practice during the year, a grade 11 refresher |
| Frisancho 2023 (Peru) | Replacing economics hours in an existing course works; teachers benefit most; about US$5–7 per pupil | A module inside social studies; ready-made workbooks; teacher training |
| Bruhn et al. 2016 (Brazil) | It works at national scale; parents benefit; credit lessons can backfire short term | Family tasks; stress the cost of credit, online loans and BNPL |
| Cannistrà et al. 2024 | Games work in Europe (+0.31 SD) | Escape rooms and the Olympiad as reinforcement |
| Compen et al. 2019 | Teacher training must be substantial and practical | 14+ hours, accredited under Regulation No. 569 |
| Cole et al. 2016 vs Urban et al. 2020 | A mandate on paper fails; real courses work | Minimum lessons and assessed outcomes in the standards |

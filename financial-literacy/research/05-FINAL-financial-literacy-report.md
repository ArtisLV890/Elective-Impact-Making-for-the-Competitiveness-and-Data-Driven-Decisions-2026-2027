# Financial literacy of young people in Latvia: compulsory on paper, missing in practice

*Final research report. Impact case for "Impact Making for the Competitiveness and Data-Driven Decisions". November 2026.*

*All Latvia figures in this version are verified against primary documents:*
- *Flash Eurobarometer 525, data annex*
- *Latvijas Banka adult survey 2022*
- *Latvijas Banka 9th-grade assessment 2025*

*The few figures still from secondary sources are marked ⚠.*

---

## 0. Ten insights in one page

1. **Latvia is joint last in the EU on financial literacy.**
   - Only **11%** of Latvian adults have a high overall financial literacy score, joint last of 27 with Portugal. The EU average is 18%, Estonia 23% and Lithuania 16%.
   - **24%** score low, the 2nd-highest share after Finland (Eurobarometer 525, 2023).
2. **Latvia's specific weakness is behaviour and resilience, not only knowledge.**
   - Latvia ranks **26th of 27** on financially savvy behaviour (52% high, against an EU average of 65%).
   - It ranks **25th of 27** on setting long-term financial goals.
   - **26%** have no emergency savings, **the highest share in the EU**. Only **19%** could live for 6+ months without income, **the lowest in the EU**.
3. **Knowledge has collapsed, and the headline index hides it.**
   - Latvijas Banka's index fell only from 63% to 61% between 2015 and 2022.
   - But the **knowledge sub-index fell from 5.1 to 3.9 out of 7**.
   - Correct answers fell on every question: compound interest **48% → 26%**, simple interest **72% → 58%**, an interest-free loan **89% → 76%**.
   - The fall was masked by better attitudes (2.0 → 2.9 out of 4).
4. **Young adults are the weakest group.**
   - **18–19-year-olds score 8.0 out of 20** and 20–29-year-olds 11.0, against 12.2 overall.
   - Only **40%** of 18–19-year-olds can calculate simple interest, against 58% of all adults.
   - The same pattern holds EU-wide: 18–24-year-olds have the lowest share with a high score (13%) and the highest share with a low score (25%).
5. **School adds little.**
   - 9th graders score **61.7%** of the maximum, the same as adults (61%).
   - 57% of 9th graders can calculate simple interest, the same as adults (58%).
   - **36% never** learn about money at school and 31% rarely; only **9%** do so often.
   - 46% learn often from the internet, and 63% from their family.
6. **Teenagers are already in the market, but don't understand it.**
   - **12%** of 9th graders have bought crypto themselves, and 28% have invested with an adult.
   - **31% think Bitcoin is legal tender** in Latvia, and only **44%** agree that a higher return means higher risk.
   - Among adults, **more own crypto (10%) than funds, stocks or bonds (9%)**. Latvia is joint last in the EU on investment products.
7. **Teenagers are targets for fraud.**
   - **48%** of 9th graders have been offered "easy money" online by strangers.
   - **18%** have been asked to pass money through their account, the money-mule pattern.
   - **16%** have already been scammed buying online, and **6%** have given away a password or PIN.
   - Nationally, fraud losses rose from **€16m (2024) to €23.7m (2025)** ⚠ (State Police). One dismantled network involved **213 money mules from Latvia, mostly young** ⚠.
8. **Teachers are part of the problem.** Latvijas Banka found teachers' knowledge of simple and compound interest **insufficient** (Brikše 2025).
9. **Latvia has no international benchmark for students.**
   - Latvia scored 501 in PISA 2018, against an OECD average of 505 and Estonia's world-best 547.
   - It **did not take part in PISA 2022's financial literacy module**, and neither did Estonia or Lithuania.
   - Between 2012 and 2018 Latvian students' scores did **not improve** (Mihno 2022).
10. **The evidence on what works is strong.**
    - School financial education raises knowledge by **0.15–0.33 SD** and behaviour by **about 0.07–0.09 SD** (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2017, 2020; Kaiser et al. 2022).
    - Large randomised trials: Brazil (+0.25 SD, 892 schools), Peru (credit records improved 3 years later), and an online game (+0.31 SD, 4 countries).
    - **Intensity, teacher training and timing** ("teachable moments") decide success.
    - A **mandate without real teaching has no effect** (Cole et al. 2016). That is Latvia's risk today.

### 1.3 9th graders: Latvijas Banka assessment 2025

*Online (CAWI), 10 Feb – 30 Apr 2025, n = 1,159 pupils in 75 schools, carried out by RAIT.*

**Index**
- **12.33 / 20 (61.7%)**, the same level as adults.
- Distribution: low 2%, medium 61%, high 37%.
- Sub-indices: knowledge 5.46/8 (51% high), behaviour and skills **4.62/8 (only 29% high)**, attitudes 2.26/4.

**Knowledge tasks (% correct)**

| Task | 9th graders | Adults 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| Divide €1,000 by 5 | 90% | 85% |
| Interest-free loan = 0% | 74% | 76% |
| 2nd pension pillar: 5% of a €1,000 salary = €50 | 61% | — |
| Inflation (2%): "buy less" | 58% | 41% (at 21%) |
| Simple interest: €100 at 2% = €102 | **57%** | 58% |
| VAT included in €121 at 21% = €21 | **41%** | — |
| Agree "higher return → higher risk" | **44%** | — |

**Misconceptions**
- **31%** think Bitcoin is legal tender in Latvia.
- **43%** think car insurance is voluntary.
- **25%** think a budget is balanced when spending exceeds income.
- **48%** don't know they join the 2nd pension pillar automatically when they start work.
- **40%** don't know how much VAT they pay.

**Products and behaviour**
- 87% have money they control; 85% have their own bank card; 80% have a bank account; 87% have shopped online.
- **12% have bought crypto themselves**; 28% have invested together with an adult.
- 80% save from pocket money (29% regularly); 62% have a savings goal.
- 55% sometimes buy things they don't need; 28% prefer spending to saving.
- 24% follow influencers' recommendations at least sometimes.

**Online risk and fraud**
- **48%** offered money-making schemes by strangers on social media.
- **18%** asked to pass money through their bank account or handle cash for a fee, the **money-mule** pattern.
- **16%** scammed when buying online.
- **11%** opened attachments from strangers, and **6%** revealed a password, card number or PIN.
- Only **15%** regularly change passwords, and **23%** think public Wi-Fi is safe for shopping.

**Where they learn about money (always or often)**

| Source | Always or often | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Family | 63% | 3% |
| Internet | 46% | 8% |
| Friends | 18% | 18% |
| **School and clubs** | **9%** | **36%** (plus 31% rarely) |

**Family**
- **73%** say only adults make money decisions at home (21% together; 5% don't know).
- 65% say money is discussed freely at home, but only **49%** plan a family budget.

### 1.4 The school system

- **Status:** financial literacy is compulsory, as a topic within social studies from grade 1 and in the upper-secondary social and civic courses (Skola2030). Before the reform it was an elective.
- **Quality:**
  - teaching is "superficial", because finance is one topic among many and teachers must cover many fields (LSM, 18.02.2026);
  - teachers' knowledge of simple and compound interest is **insufficient** (Latvijas Banka teacher assessment, presented by Brikše in 2025);
  - funding for learning materials is under pressure in the multi-year budget (LSM, 02.06.2026).
- **Benchmarking:**
  - PISA 2018 financial literacy: Latvia **501**, OECD average **505**, Estonia **547** (best).
  - Latvia **did not take part in PISA 2022's financial literacy module**, which had 20 participants and no Baltic state.
  - PISA 2012 → 2018 showed **no improvement** (Mihno 2022).
  - Results depend strongly on maths and reading skills, and on socioeconomic background (Mihno 2021).
  - Latvian students struggle most with tasks that require justifying a decision (Kiseļova & Mihno 2018).
- **Strategy:**
  - National Strategy 2021–2027, coordinated by Latvijas Banka.
  - The strategy itself says the 2014–2020 indicators lacked clear principles and targets.
  - Two strategies later, knowledge is lower than in 2015.

### 1.5 Why it matters (the stakes)

- **Household resilience and pensions.**
  - Latvia has the EU's highest share with no emergency savings, and the lowest share able to last 6 months without income.
  - Low literacy is linked to lower wealth, less stock market participation and worse retirement planning (Lusardi & Mitchell 2014, 2023; van Rooij, Lusardi & Alessie 2011; Lusardi, Michaud & Mitchell 2017).
- **Fraud.**
  - Teenagers are recruited as money mules; an 18% exposure rate among 15-year-olds.
  - National fraud losses are rising (€23.7m in 2025 ⚠).
- **Capital markets.** The EU's Savings and Investments Union and its Financial Literacy Strategy (COM(2025) 681, 30 Sept 2025) aim to turn savers into investors. Latvia starts from joint-last investment participation.
- **Timing.**
  - Latvia's national strategy ends in **2027**.
  - The EU mid-term review of the Savings and Investments Union is in **2027**.
  - The budget for learning materials is being decided now.

## 2. What does research say?

### 2.1 Causes (diagnosis)

| Cause | Latvian evidence | International evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Low intensity**: finance diluted within social studies | 36% never learn about money at school; only 9% often | Effects grow with intensity, with diminishing returns (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2017, 2020) |
| **Teacher knowledge** | Teachers' interest knowledge insufficient | Teacher professional development is key but under-studied (Compen, De Witte & Schelfhout 2019) |
| **Weak link to real decisions** | Teens already hold cards, crypto and online accounts, but learn from the internet and influencers | "Teachable moments": education tied to an immediate decision works best (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2017; Fernandes et al. 2014) |
| **Family socialisation** | 73% of 9th graders are not involved in money decisions; involvement predicts higher scores | Family and school are complementary channels (Grohmann, Kouwenberg & Menkhoff 2015) |
| **Inequality** | Basic education 9.2 vs higher 13.4; low income 11.2 vs high 14.0 | Gaps by socioeconomic status persist (Lusardi, Mitchell & Curto 2010; Mihno 2021) |
| **No measurement loop** | No PISA financial literacy since 2018; weak strategy indicators | — |

### 2.2 Solutions and their expected impact

**Meta-analyses**

| Study | Coverage | Knowledge | Behaviour | Key moderators |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer (2014), *Management Science* | 201 studies | — | 0.1% of variance | Effects decay over time, so just-in-time education is better |
| Kaiser & Menkhoff (2017), *World Bank Economic Review* | 126 studies | 0.26 SD | 0.09 SD (0.08 in RCTs) | **Intensity** and **teachable moments** matter; weaker for low-income groups; borrowing is harder to change than saving; mandatory programmes possibly weaker |
| Kaiser & Menkhoff (2020), *Economics of Education Review* | 37 school experiments | **0.33 SD** (RCTs 0.15) | **0.07 SD** | Intensity, with diminishing returns |
| Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban (2022), *Journal of Financial Economics* | 76 RCTs, 160,000+ people | Positive | Positive | Comparable to education interventions in other fields; robust to publication bias |

**Key school trials and natural experiments**

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Bruhn et al. (2016), *AEJ: Applied* | RCT, 892 schools, ~25,000 students, Brazil | Proficiency +0.25 SD; more saving and budgeting; spillovers to parents; *but* more expensive consumer credit |
| Frisancho (2020), IDB | RCT, 300 schools, Peru | Knowledge +0.15 SD; better credit records 3 years later; poorer parents' default probability −26% |
| Cannistrà et al. (2024), *Journal of Comparative Economics* | RCT, 2,220 students, 4 countries; an online escape-room game | **+0.313 SD** |
| Lührmann, Serra-Garcia & Winter (2015), *Journal of Banking & Finance* | German low-SES schools; 90-minute sessions by volunteer professionals | More interest and knowledge (especially risk); fewer impulse purchases; no change in saving |
| Urban, Schmeiser, Collins & Brown (2020), *Economics of Education Review* | US state mandates with dedicated courses; difference-in-differences with credit data | Higher credit scores, lower delinquency, with a lag |
| Cole, Paulson & Shastry (2016), *Journal of Human Resources* | Earlier US mandates | **No effect** of personal finance mandates; **maths** training helped |
| Batty, Collins & Odders-White (2015), *Journal of Consumer Affairs* | US elementary schools | Knowledge and attitudes improved |

**Design principles from the evidence**
1. A dedicated, **intensive** block of lessons rather than scattered mentions.
2. **Trained teachers.**
3. Content tied to the **real decisions teenagers face now**: cards, online shopping, crypto, fraud, first salary and the 2nd pension pillar.
4. **Game-based and short, repeated formats**.
5. **Involving parents**.
6. **Measure** results, and expect knowledge to move before behaviour.


![Figure 7. Effects of school financial education on knowledge, key studies](reports/figures/school_finance_education_effects.png)

### 2.3 Expected impact (illustrative)

- **Knowledge.** Moving 9th graders by +0.15 to +0.30 SD would roughly close a third to half of the gap between Latvia's 9th-grade knowledge level and the "high" band.
  - It would plausibly bring the simple-interest share from 57% to 65–70%.
  - This is an illustration based on the meta-analytic effect sizes, not a forecast.
- **Behaviour.** Expect +0.07 to +0.09 SD, visible over years, not months. Credit effects appeared with a lag in the US (Urban et al. 2020) and Peru (Frisancho 2020).
- **Fraud.** There is no causal estimate for money-mule recruitment. Treat it as a monitored outcome, using the 9th-grade survey question on mule approaches.

## 3. What should be done

**Goal:** by 2030, make compulsory financial education real: taught intensively, by trained teachers, linked to real decisions, and measured.

1. **A ready-to-teach module.**
   - 10–12 lessons in grade 9 and again in grade 11, aligned with the EU/OECD competence framework for children and youth.
   - Topics: interest and compounding, inflation, risk and return, cards and online payments, crypto versus legal tender, fraud and money mules, first salary, taxes and the 2nd pension pillar.
   - **Owners:** IZM and VISC, with Latvijas Banka.
2. **Teacher professional development.**
   - A short certified course for social studies teachers, focused on the weak spots: interest, compounding and risk.
   - Designed on Compen et al. (2019): content focus, active learning, duration, collaboration.
   - **Owners:** Latvijas Banka, VISC and universities.
3. **Gamified, repeated activities spread through the year.**
   - Classroom games (an escape-room format); an Open Economics Olympiad and Baltic competitions.
   - Volunteer "finance coaches" from banks (Finance Latvia).
   - **Partners:** Latvijas Banka, banks, and NGOs such as Jaunie Ekonomisti and Junior Achievement.
4. **Family tasks.** Homework that involves parents in a real household money decision, such as a budget or comparing offers. This targets the 73% of pupils who are excluded from these decisions.
5. **Measurement.**
   - Repeat Latvijas Banka's 9th-grade assessment every 2 years.
   - Report the adult survey's 18–29 age group with a larger youth sample.
   - **Rejoin the PISA financial literacy module** in the next cycle that offers it ⚠.
   - Put clear indicators into the **post-2027 national strategy**.

**KPIs (baseline → target, 2030)**

| KPI | Baseline | Target 2030 |
|---|---|---|
| 9th-grade index | 12.33 (2025) | 14.0 |
| 9th graders who calculate simple interest | 57% (2025) | 75% |
| 9th graders who "never" learn about money at school | 36% (2025) | < 10% |
| 9th graders who know Bitcoin is not legal tender | 69% (2025) | 90% |
| Adult knowledge sub-index | 3.9 / 7 (2022) | Back to 2015 level (5.1) |
| Ages 20–29 overall index | 11.0 (2022) | ≥ 12.2 (adult average) |
| Teachers passing the core-concepts check | Baseline from Latvijas Banka assessment | 80% |
| PISA financial literacy | Not participating | Participating |

# Part B. Evidence for the solution: newer data, curriculum, existing activity, Estonia, costs and policy window


## B1. Is there newer adult data than 2022?

**Yes: the OECD/INFE 2023 international survey,** with data from 2022–2023 and 39 countries, as reported by Latvijas Banka:

| Indicator | Latvia | OECD average |
|---|---|---|
| Overall financial literacy (% of maximum) | **59%** | **63%** |
| Adults reaching the minimum target score | **46%** | **58%** |
| Digital financial literacy | 46% | 55% |
| Victim of financial fraud in the past 2 years | **18%** | — (described as above average) |

**What this means for the case**
- The 2023 international result (59%) is **lower** than Latvijas Banka's own 2022 figure (61%). So the low level is confirmed by a second, independent survey.
- **Fewer than half of Latvian adults (46%) reach the OECD's minimum target**, against 58% across the OECD. This is a strong and simple headline.
- **18% of adults have been victims of financial fraud in two years**, which links directly to the fraud findings among teenagers.

**What was not found**
- No newer Latvijas Banka adult index than 2022.
- Latvijas Banka's 2025 slides mention a 2024 *teacher* survey and the 2025 9th-grade survey, not a new adult survey.
- Latvijas Banka's own page confirms that "financial knowledge has declined over seven years, weakest among 18–19-year-olds and people with lower education", while attitudes improved.

**Why did knowledge fall?** No official explanation was found. Plausible factors:
1. The 2022 inflation question used 21% inflation, so that item isn't fully comparable.
2. Fieldwork took place during the 2022 energy and inflation shock.
3. A genuine decline. Every item fell, including simple division (93% → 85%), which suggests part of the drop could be a survey effect.

**Recommendation:** present it as "knowledge has fallen on every question since 2015, and the OECD 2023 survey confirms the low level". Ask Latvijas Banka for its interpretation, which also makes a good interview question.

## B3. What the curriculum actually requires

**What we know**
- Financial literacy is **compulsory, but not a separate subject**. It is part of **social studies (sociālās zinības) from grade 1**, and of the upper-secondary social and civic courses (general, optional and advanced levels).
- **Before Skola2030**, "economics and financial literacy" was an **elective** taken by some pupils only.
- Grade 7–9 learning outcomes include explaining how resources are allocated and how the market works, and **making responsible decisions by evaluating services offered by financial institutions**.
- Skola2030 provides a "**Financial literacy**" assessment criteria sheet for social studies lessons, and textbook units on banks and money.
- **Quality:** LSM (18.02.2026) reports "superficial" teaching because:
  - finance is one of many social studies topics;
  - teachers must be experts in many fields;
  - finance professionals rarely visit.

  More materials are planned, but funding for learning materials is under budget pressure (LSM, 02.06.2026).

**What was not found:** the number of lessons devoted to finance. The standard sets total social studies hours, not hours per topic. This is itself a finding: **no minimum number of finance lessons is guaranteed**, so delivery depends on each teacher.

**Action:** one or two short interviews with social studies teachers: "How many lessons per year actually cover money and finance?"

## B4. What already exists in Latvia (activity is high, results are flat)

| Initiative | Reach |
|---|---|
| National strategy partners | **22 partners, 115+ activities in 2024**; **230+ activities** since 2021 |
| Latvijas Banka's own sessions | ~**2,000 people** in 2024, rated 4.8/5 |
| "Naudas diena" (Money Day) for schools | **510 schools, 78%** of general and vocational schools (2023) |
| Financial Literacy Week | Annual, nationwide |
| SEB School Ambassadors | Pupils teach pupils in schools across Latvia (5+ years) |
| Junior Achievement Latvia | A record ~**1,500 student companies** (2023) |
| Latvijas Banka school material "Manu ekonomiku"; adult programme "Kļūsti par naudas pratēju" (with IZM) | — |
| **New cooperation memorandum, 16 March 2026** | **28 organisations**. Priorities to 2027: **pupils and teachers**, investment culture, responsible borrowing, research |

**Key insight: Latvia is rich in activity but poor in results.**
- Hundreds of events, and 78% of schools reached on Money Day.
- Yet knowledge fell from 2015 to 2022, and 36% of 9th graders say they never learn about money at school.
- The strategy counts **activities** (partners, events, participants), not **learning outcomes**.
- The 2021–2027 strategy itself admits that its earlier indicators lacked clear principles and targets.
- This fits the international evidence: one-off events are low-intensity, and **intensity** is what drives impact (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2017, 2020).

**This lets us reframe the solution:** Latvia doesn't need more events. It needs to turn existing activity into **intensive, assessed classroom learning** delivered by **trained teachers**.

## B5. How Estonia does it (and what to be careful about)

**The model**
- A **national strategy "Money Smart Estonia" 2021–2030**, led by the **Ministry of Finance**, with the Ministry of Education and partners including the NGO Rahatarkus.
- **Teachers.** An Estonian 2020 study found **many teachers lacked formal training** in financial education. In response:
  - **financial literacy has been part of a six-month training programme for entrepreneurship teachers since 2019**;
  - materials and games are shared online for all school stages.
- **Integration with entrepreneurship education.** The Ministry of Education's **"Edu ja Tegu"** programme (since 2016) trains teachers and provides methods and materials:
  - teachers from **58%** of education institutions had taken part by 2022;
  - entrepreneurship education was implemented in **71%** of general schools (2021);
  - financial literacy is embedded in it.
- **Scale:** 506 general schools, ~17,500 teachers, ~164,000 pupils.
- **Results:** PISA 2018 financial literacy **547, the best in the world**. Among 18–29-year-olds, **91% save** in some form (Estonian Ministry of Finance, 2024).

**Caution**
- Estonia also leads Europe in PISA maths and reading.
- Financial literacy scores depend heavily on maths and reading (OECD; Mihno 2021).
- Its lead is therefore **not only** the result of financial education.

**What to borrow from Estonia (in our recommendations)**
- **Systematic teacher training**, integrated into an existing teacher programme.
- **Ready-made materials for every school stage.**
- **Linking finance to entrepreneurship education.**
- **A ministry-level owner.**

**Lithuania, for completeness**
- A **Public Financial Education Action Plan 2024–2028**, coordinated by a council chaired by the Ministry of Finance (with the central bank and Junior Achievement).
- A pilot of a **dedicated financial literacy lesson from grade 1** (about 2,400 children), aiming to embed a lesson in the national curriculum and train teachers. ⚠ Status unclear.

## B6. Rough cost of the proposal (illustrative)

**Benchmarks from the research**
- **Peru** (Frisancho): **US$6.6 per student**, including workbooks, distribution, **teacher training** and monitoring.
- **Brazil** (Bruhn et al.): **BRL 206 per student**. Students' lower use of expensive credit saves about BRL 46 a year, so the programme **pays for itself in about 5 years**.

**Latvia: assumptions** (to be checked with IZM and VISC)
- **About 640 general schools** (643 in 2023, CSP).
- **About 20,000 pupils per grade cohort**, based on ~19,000 births a year in 2010–2011 ⚠.
- **About 1,000 social studies teachers** teaching grades 7–12, roughly 1.5 per school ⚠.

| Item | Assumption | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Develop the module (grades 9 and 11), digital, based on existing Latvijas Banka and Skola2030 material | One-off | **€50–100k** |
| Teacher training (36-hour certified course) | ~1,000 teachers × €150–250 | **€150–250k one-off** |
| Delivery: materials, games, competitions, volunteer coaches | ~20,000 pupils × €5–10 per year | **€100–200k a year** |
| Measurement | Latvijas Banka 9th-grade survey every 2 years (already exists) | Within current budgets |
| **Total** | | **About €0.2–0.35m set-up + €0.1–0.2m a year** |

**Perspective**
- National fraud losses were **€23.7m in 2025** ⚠ (State Police), and 18% of adults were fraud victims in two years (OECD/INFE).
- The programme costs about **1% of one year's fraud losses** a year.
- The Brazilian evidence suggests it can **pay for itself through lower borrowing costs within about 5 years**.
- Partners (Latvijas Banka, commercial banks via Finance Latvia, and EU funds such as ESF+) could cover part of it.

*These are back-of-envelope figures to show the order of magnitude, not a budget.*

## Appendix A. Methodology and frameworks

- **Definitions and frameworks:**
  - OECD/INFE: financial literacy = knowledge + behaviour + attitudes. This structures both the Latvijas Banka indices and the OECD survey.
  - The EU/OECD financial competence frameworks for children, youth and adults.
  - Lusardi & Mitchell's human-capital view and their "Big Three" questions.
  - Kaiser & Menkhoff's intensity and teachable-moment design lens.
  - The family-and-school socialisation model (Grohmann et al. 2015).
- **Data:**
  - Flash Eurobarometer 525 (online panel, quota sample, about 1,000 per country). Ranks computed from the data annex by the authors.
  - Latvijas Banka 2022 (face-to-face, random stratified, n = 1,007).
  - Latvijas Banka 2025 9th-grade assessment (online, 75 schools, n = 1,159).
  - OECD PISA 2018 and 2022 Volume IV.
- **Limitations:**
  - The Eurobarometer is an online panel, so it may overstate digital populations.
  - The 18–19 subgroup in the 2022 survey is about 30 respondents (indicative only), but 20–29-year-olds show the same pattern.
  - The 2022 inflation question changed its parameter, so the trends are indicative.
  - The 9th-grade results are a single cross-section (no trend yet).
  - Most causal evidence is from outside the Baltics; Cannistrà et al. (2024) is the closest.
  - Jaunie Ekonomisti's own Olympiad data is used **only** as illustration (non-representative).

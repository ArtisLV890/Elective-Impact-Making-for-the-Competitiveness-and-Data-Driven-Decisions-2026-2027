# Financial literacy in Latvia: first research pass

*Impact case pivot, November 2026. Status: first pass. Figures marked ⚠ still need checking against the original report.*

## 1. Headline facts

### Adults: low and not improving
- **Latvijas Banka's adult financial literacy index is 12.2 out of 20 (2022) and roughly unchanged since 2015** (Latvijas Banka, Brikše, Saeima conference presentation, September 2025). This is despite the National Strategy for Financial Literacy 2021–2027.
- **Digital financial literacy is 46% of the maximum score** (Latvijas Banka survey, end of 2022).
- **Eurobarometer** (Flash Eurobarometer 525, 2023, all 27 EU countries): Latvia has the **second-largest share of people with a low overall financial literacy score, 24%**. Only Finland is higher (27%), followed by Belgium (22%). ⚠ Check Latvia's "high score" share in the report's country chart.
- **OECD/INFE 2023** (39 countries): Latvia took part. The cross-country average is 60/100 and the OECD average is 63/100. ⚠ Latvia's own score is in the report's Annex. In the earlier 2016 round, Latvia and Estonia had above-average *knowledge* but low overall literacy because of weak *behaviour* scores.

### Financial resilience: Latvia trails its Baltic neighbours
- **25% of Latvians have no savings at all, and only 21% have a cushion of at least 3 months' salary** (Swedbank Institute of Finance, February 2025, n = 1,000).
- **Regular savers** (saving more than 10% of income): **Latvia 14%, Estonia 17%, Lithuania 23%**. Also, 43% of Latvians save only irregularly and 17% have never saved for unforeseen expenses (Luminor/Norstat, September 2025, n = 3,002 across the Baltics).
- **Saving rises with income:** 9% of those earning up to €550 a month save regularly, against about a quarter of those earning €1,250–1,500 (Luminor 2025).
- **Retirement readiness is lowest in the Baltics: 3.2/10**, against 3.5 in Lithuania and 3.6 in Estonia (SEB survey).
  - Rupeika-Apoga & Priede (2025, *Risks*) link financial literacy, product ownership and confidence in advisers to retirement confidence in the Baltics, using Eurobarometer data.
  - The IMF (October 2025) urged Latvians to save more for retirement.

### Young people: the school gap
- **9th graders score 12.3 out of 20** (Latvijas Banka, February–April 2025):
  - **36%** say they have *never* learned about money at school or in clubs, and **31%** only rarely;
  - **46%** get money information from the internet "always or often", against **9%** from school;
  - **79%** are mostly *not involved* in family money decisions, and those who are involved score higher;
  - **48%** have been offered online ways to "earn money", and **18%** have been approached to become **money mules**;
  - girls score lower than boys, and pupils in Vidzeme and Kurzeme score lower than elsewhere.
- **Recent school leavers know the least about personal finance of any group** (Latvijas Banka adult survey, reported by LSM, February 2026).
- **On paper, financial literacy is compulsory.** Since the Skola2030 reform it is taught in social studies from grade 1 and in upper-secondary social and civic courses. Before that, economics and financial literacy were an elective (Skola2030).
- **In practice it is taught "superficially"** (LSM, 18 February 2026). The reasons given:
  - it is one of many topics in social studies;
  - teachers must cover many fields;
  - finance specialists rarely come into schools.

  More teaching materials are planned, but the multi-year budget proposes cutting funding for learning materials (LSM, June 2026).

### Comparison: Estonia
- Estonia has a "Money Smart Estonia" strategy for 2021–2030, coordinated by the Ministry of Finance and implemented in schools by the Ministry of Education. It aims for financial education from kindergarten to university, with guidance materials for teachers at each school stage.
- **PISA 2022 ranked Estonian students the most financially literate in the world** (Estonian Ministry of Education). ⚠ Check whether Latvia took part in the PISA 2022 financial literacy assessment; we found no Latvian score.

## 2. What research says about solutions

| Approach | Evidence | Size of effect |
|---|---|---|
| **School financial education** | Kaiser & Menkhoff (2020), *Economics of Education Review*: meta-analysis of 37 (quasi-)experiments | Knowledge **+0.33 SD**, behaviour **+0.07 SD**. Randomised trials only: knowledge 0.15 SD, behaviour 0.07 SD. More intensive programmes work better, with diminishing returns |
| **Financial education in general** | Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban (2022), *Journal of Financial Economics* 145: meta-analysis of **76 randomised experiments, over 160,000 people** | Positive causal effects on knowledge and behaviour, similar in size to education interventions in other fields; robust to publication bias |
| **The sceptical view** | Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer (2014), *Management Science* 60(8): meta-analysis of 201 studies | Education explains only **0.1% of the variance** in financial behaviour, and effects **decay over time**. This argues for "just-in-time" education close to the decision |
| **School mandates (US)** | Urban, Schmeiser, Collins & Brown (2020), *Economics of Education Review*; Brown et al. (2014/2016) on credit and debt | State requirements for personal finance courses improved young adults' financial behaviour. ⚠ Check effect sizes |
| **Defaults and nudges** | Madrian & Shea (2001); Thaler & Benartzi (2004), "Save More Tomorrow" | Automatic enrolment and commitment savings change behaviour without needing knowledge. A complement to education |

**Implications:**
1. Education raises **knowledge** reliably, but **behaviour** only modestly, so it needs to be **intensive**, **timed close to real decisions** and **combined with nudges**.
2. Latvia's specific weakness is **behaviour** (OECD/INFE 2016) and **savings**, not just knowledge. That points to behavioural, just-in-time tools.

## 3. Possible problem framings for the impact case

Choose **one**, because a narrow case is stronger.

1. **"Compulsory on paper, missing in practice": school financial education.** ⭐ My recommendation.
   - Owners: IZM, VISC, Latvijas Banka.
   - Evidence: 36% never learned about money at school, school leavers are the weakest group, teaching is "superficial", and the materials budget is under threat.
   - Solutions: teacher training, a ready-made module, finance professionals in classrooms, a family component.
   - Strongest evidence base (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020) and a clear owner.
2. **"A nation without a safety cushion": adult savings and resilience.**
   - 25% have no savings, and Latvia has the fewest regular savers in the Baltics.
   - Solutions: savings defaults (e.g. via employers or banks), just-in-time education, the third pension pillar.
   - Higher stakes, but harder to own.
3. **"The money-mule generation": youth and online financial fraud.**
   - 18% of 9th graders have been approached to become money mules.
   - Solutions: digital financial literacy, bank and school partnerships.
   - Very media-friendly, but narrower.

## 4. Frameworks for the appendix (to develop)

- **OECD/INFE definition:** financial literacy = knowledge + behaviour + attitudes. This is also the structure of Latvijas Banka's index.
- **Lusardi & Mitchell's "Big Three" questions:** interest, inflation and risk diversification.
- **Behavioural economics:** present bias, defaults and commitment devices (Thaler & Benartzi; Madrian & Shea).
- **Policy design:** "just-in-time" education versus broad education (Fernandes et al. 2014).

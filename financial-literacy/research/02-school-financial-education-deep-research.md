# Compulsory on paper, missing in practice: school financial education in Latvia

*Deep research report. Impact case: Financial literacy in Latvia. November 2026.*

> **How to read this report**
> - Every figure has a source. Figures marked ⚠ come from secondary reporting and should be checked against the original document before the final submission (see the evidence register in Appendix C).
> - Data from Jaunie Ekonomisti's own Olympiad survey is **not** used as evidence. It appears only as illustration, because it is not a representative sample.

---

## Executive summary

- **The problem.** Latvia made financial literacy a compulsory part of the school curriculum (Skola2030, within social studies). But young Latvians leave school among the least financially literate people in the country.
  - Latvijas Banka's index gives **18–19-year-olds 8.0 out of 20**, the lowest of any age group, against 12.2 for adults overall (2022).
  - **36%** of 9th graders say they have never learned about money at school, and another **31%** only rarely (Latvijas Banka 2025).
  - Teachers themselves have **insufficient knowledge of simple and compound interest** (Latvijas Banka 2025).
- **Why it matters.**
  - Latvia's adults have the **lowest share with high financial literacy in the EU (11%)** ⚠, the **lowest share able to cover six months of expenses (19%)** ⚠, and the fewest regular savers in the Baltics (**14%**, against 17% in Estonia and 23% in Lithuania).
  - Financial literacy is linked to wealth accumulation, stock market participation and retirement readiness (Lusardi & Mitchell 2014; van Rooij et al. 2011; Lusardi et al. 2017).
  - Young Latvians are also targets for fraud: **18% of 9th graders have been approached to become money mules**.
- **What the evidence says works.**
  - School financial education raises knowledge by **0.33 SD** and behaviour by **0.07 SD** on average (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020). In 76 randomised trials, effects are real and comparable to other education interventions (Kaiser et al. 2022).
  - Large school RCTs:
    - Brazil: **+0.25 SD**, 892 schools (Bruhn et al. 2016);
    - Peru: 300 schools, with effects on later **credit records** (Frisancho 2020);
    - a four-country online game, including Estonia: **+0.31 SD** (Cannistrà et al. 2024).
  - Implementation quality, above all **teacher training**, decides whether a mandate works. A US mandate without real teaching had no effect (Cole et al. 2016). Mandates with dedicated courses improved credit scores and cut delinquency (Urban et al. 2020).
- **Our proposal (to be refined).** Make the compulsory topic real, through:
  1. **a ready-to-teach, assessed financial literacy module** for grades 8–12;
  2. **teacher professional development** on the core concepts (interest, inflation, risk);
  3. **regular short, gamified activities**, including competitions, in partnership with Latvijas Banka and NGOs;
  4. **a family component**, because 79% of 9th graders are not involved in household money decisions.

  Measure it with the instruments that already exist: Latvijas Banka's 9th-grade index and the age 18–24 band of the adult index.

## 1. What is the problem and why is it important?

### 1.1 Latvian adults: low financial literacy that is not improving

| Indicator | Latvia | Comparison | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Latvijas Banka adult financial literacy index (2022) | **12.2 / 20** | Unchanged since 2015 | Latvijas Banka 2022 survey (n = 1,007, OECD/INFE method); Brikše 2025 |
| OECD/INFE overall score | **59% of maximum** | OECD average 63% | Latvijas Banka, citing OECD/INFE ⚠ |
| Share with a **high** financial literacy score (2023) | **11%** ⚠ | EU: "less than a fifth" | Flash Eurobarometer 525; Alpha Architect summary; EU Financial Literacy Strategy 2025 |
| Able to cover **6+ months** of expenses | **19%, lowest in the EU** ⚠ | — | Flash Eurobarometer 525, via Demertzis et al. 2024 |
| Own stocks, funds or bonds | **9%, tied last in the EU** ⚠ | More own crypto (10%) | Flash Eurobarometer 525 (quoted in the Jaunie Ekonomisti deck; to be verified) |
| No savings at all | **25%** | Only 21% have 3+ months' salary | Swedbank Institute of Finance, Feb 2025 (n = 1,000) |
| Save regularly (>10% of income) | **14%** | Estonia 17%, Lithuania 23% | Luminor/Norstat, Sept 2025 (n = 3,002) |
| Retirement readiness (1–10) | **3.2** | Estonia 3.6, Lithuania 3.5 | SEB survey |
| Digital financial literacy | **4.6 / 10** | — | Latvijas Banka, end of 2022 |

**Interpretation.**
- In the earlier OECD/INFE 2016 round, Latvia had above-average financial *knowledge* but low overall literacy because of weak *behaviour*.
- Latvijas Banka's 2022 survey found that knowledge had *declined* since 2015, while attitudes and saving and borrowing habits improved, leaving the index flat.
- So the problem is not only knowledge, but knowledge is weakening exactly where schooling should help.

### 1.2 Young people: the weakest group

- **18–19-year-olds score 8.0 / 20**, the lowest of any age group. They score about **3 out of 7** on the knowledge component, and **only 40% can answer a simple interest question** (Latvijas Banka 2022 survey, as reported) ⚠.
- **Recent school leavers know the least about personal finance** of any group (Latvijas Banka survey, reported by LSM, 18.02.2026).
- **9th graders score 12.3 / 20** (Latvijas Banka, Feb–Apr 2025, n = 1,159):
  - **36%** never learned about money at school or in clubs, and **31%** rarely;
  - **46%** get money information from the internet "always or often", against **9%** from school;
  - **79%** are mostly not involved in family money decisions, and those who are involved score higher;
  - **48%** have been offered online ways to "earn money", and **18%** have been approached to become **money mules**;
  - girls score lower than boys, and pupils in Vidzeme and Kurzeme score lower than elsewhere.
- **PISA:**
  - In 2018, Latvia scored **501**, close to the OECD average (505) and far behind Estonia (**547, best in the world**). Only 10.6% of Latvian students were below the baseline level, against 14.7% in the OECD (IZM).
  - Comparing PISA 2012 and 2018, Mihno (2022) finds **no improvement** in Latvian students' financial literacy, despite school and national efforts.
  - PISA scores depend heavily on reading and maths ability, and on socioeconomic background (Mihno 2021).
  - Latvia does not appear among the 20 participants in PISA 2022's financial literacy module ⚠ (Volume IV participant list to be checked), so Latvia currently has **no international benchmark for students after 2018**.

### 1.3 The school system: compulsory, but "superficial"

- **Before Skola2030**, economics and financial literacy were an **elective** that only some pupils took. **Now** financial literacy is part of **social studies from grade 1** and of the upper-secondary social and civic courses, so formally every pupil learns it (Skola2030).
- **In practice** it is taught **"superficially"** (LSM, 18.02.2026), because:
  1. it is one of many topics within social studies;
  2. social studies teachers must cover many fields;
  3. finance professionals rarely come into schools.
- **Teachers.** Latvijas Banka's 2024 assessment of economics and finance teachers found that **their knowledge of simple and compound interest is insufficient**, which "may affect their ability to teach topics such as investment, savings, pensions and borrowing" (Brikše, Latvijas Banka, Saeima conference, Sept 2025).
- **Money.** More teaching materials are planned, but the multi-year budget proposes **cutting funding for learning materials** (LSM, 02.06.2026).
- **Strategy.** The National Strategy for Financial Literacy 2021–2027 (coordinated by Latvijas Banka since 2022) itself notes that the 2014–2020 strategy **lacked clear principles for indicators and target values**. Despite two strategies, the adult index has not moved since 2015.

### 1.4 Why it matters now

1. **Household resilience and pensions.** Latvia trails its Baltic neighbours on savings and retirement readiness, and the IMF (Oct 2025) urged Latvians to save more for retirement. Financial literacy explains a sizeable part of wealth differences:
   - Lusardi, Michaud & Mitchell (2017, *JPE*) model it as a driver of wealth inequality;
   - van Rooij, Lusardi & Alessie (2011, *JFE*) show that people with low literacy are much less likely to invest in stocks.
2. **Fraud and money mules.** With 18% of 9th graders already approached, digital financial literacy is a security issue as well as an economic one.
3. **EU policy window.** On **30 September 2025** the European Commission adopted an **EU Financial Literacy Strategy** (COM(2025) 681) as part of the **Savings and Investments Union**, together with a blueprint for Savings and Investment Accounts. The Commission says less than a fifth of EU citizens have high financial literacy. The joint **EU/OECD financial competence frameworks for children, youth and adults** provide ready-made learning outcomes. A mid-term review of the Savings and Investments Union is due in **2027**, the same year Latvia's national strategy ends.
4. **Latvia's national strategy expires in 2027.** The next strategy is being prepared, so now is the time to put school delivery at its centre.

## 2. What do research and data say?

### 2.1 How big is the problem?
See section 1.

**Summary:** Latvia's adults are at or near the **bottom of the EU**. Young adults are the **weakest group in Latvia**. School teaching reaches a minority in practice. Teachers lack confidence in core concepts.

### 2.2 Why does the problem exist? (causes)

| Cause | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Low intensity and dilution.** Finance is one topic among many in social studies | LSM 2026; Kaiser & Menkhoff (2020) show that **more intensive** programmes have larger effects, with diminishing returns |
| **Teacher knowledge and confidence** | Latvijas Banka teacher assessment (2024/25). The international literature finds teacher professional development is a key, under-studied ingredient (Compen, De Witte & Schelfhout 2019, *Educational Research Review*) |
| **Abstract and irrelevant content.** Students turn to the internet | 46% internet vs 9% school (Latvijas Banka 2025). Latvian students struggle most with analytical, justify-your-reasoning tasks (Kiseļova & Mihno 2018) |
| **Family socialisation is missing** | 79% of 9th graders not involved in money decisions. Family and school act through **complementary channels** (Grohmann, Kouwenberg & Menkhoff 2015, *J. Economic Psychology*) |
| **Inequality** | Socioeconomic status, gender and region predict scores (Mihno 2021; Latvijas Banka 2025) |
| **No benchmark or feedback loop** | Latvia appears to have no PISA financial literacy data after 2018 ⚠. The strategy's indicators were weak (Latvijas Banka strategy document) |

### 2.3 What solutions exist, and what is their expected impact?

**A. Meta-analyses: the size of the effect**

| Study | Scope | Knowledge | Behaviour | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Kaiser & Menkhoff (2020)**, *Economics of Education Review* 78 | 37 school (quasi-)experiments | **+0.33 SD** | **+0.07 SD** | Randomised trials only: 0.15 / 0.07 SD. More intensive programmes work better, with diminishing returns |
| **Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban (2022)**, *Journal of Financial Economics* 145 | **76 RCTs**, >160,000 people | Positive | Positive | Effects comparable to other education interventions; robust to publication bias; >5× larger than Fernandes et al. estimated |
| **Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer (2014)**, *Management Science* 60(8) | 201 studies | — | Explains **0.1%** of variance | The sceptical benchmark: effects **decay** over time, so "just-in-time" education is recommended |

**B. Large school-based randomised trials**

| Study | Country / design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Bruhn et al. (2016)**, *AEJ: Applied* 8(4) | Brazil, **892 schools, ~25,000 students**, RCT | Financial proficiency **+0.25 SD**. More saving and budgeting, and **spillovers to parents**. But also more use of expensive consumer credit, a warning that content matters |
| **Frisancho (2020)**, IDB / *Economics of Education Review* ⚠ | Peru, **300 public high schools**, RCT | Exit-exam financial literacy **+0.15 SD**. Long-lasting effects on **credit records three years later**. For parents in poorer households: default probability −26%, credit scores +5% |
| **Cannistrà, De Beckker, Agasisti, Amagir, Põder, Vartiak & De Witte (2024)**, *Journal of Comparative Economics* 52(4) | **2,220 students, four countries** (including Estonia ⚠), RCT of an **online game** ("Financial Escape Room") | Financial literacy **+0.313 SD**. Shows that a short, gamified, low-cost intervention can work |
| **Lührmann, Serra-Garcia & Winter (2015)**, *Journal of Banking & Finance* 54 | Germany, low-SES school tracks; **90-minute sessions run by volunteer finance professionals** | More interest and knowledge (especially assessing risk), fewer impulse purchases; no significant effect on saving |
| **Batty, Collins & Odders-White (2015)**, *Journal of Consumer Affairs* 49(1) | US elementary schools | Knowledge and attitudes improved |
| **Kalwij et al. (2019)**, *Journal of Consumer Affairs* | Dutch primary schools ("Money Week") | Knowledge improved ⚠ |

**C. Evidence from school mandates (natural experiments)**

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Urban, Schmeiser, Collins & Brown (2020)**, *Economics of Education Review* 78 | US states (Georgia, Idaho, Texas) introducing personal finance requirements, compared with neighbouring states; credit-bureau data | **Higher credit scores and lower delinquency** among young adults, appearing with a lag. Texas delinquency −6 percentage points (−33%) ⚠ |
| **Cole, Paulson & Shastry (2016)**, *Journal of Human Resources* 51(3) | Earlier US mandates | **No effect** of personal finance mandates, but **extra maths training** improved financial outcomes. The lesson: a mandate on paper, without dedicated, well-taught courses, does not work, **which is Latvia's risk** |

**D. Teachers and families**
- **Teacher professional development.** Compen, De Witte & Schelfhout (2019) identify the features of effective teacher training: content focus, active learning, duration, coherence, collaboration and ownership. They note that few studies test it rigorously. Their follow-up work finds that student achievement improves **only when teachers actually engage** with the training.
- **Family.** Family and school have complementary effects (Grohmann et al. 2015). School programmes can spill over to parents (Bruhn et al. 2016; Frisancho, parents' credit outcomes).

**E. Benchmark country: Estonia**
- **PISA 2018: 547, the best in the world.**
- "Money Smart Estonia" strategy 2021–2030, coordinated by the Ministry of Finance and implemented in schools by the Ministry of Education. It aims for financial education from kindergarten to university, with **teacher guidance materials for each school stage**.

**What this means for expected impact in Latvia.** Illustration, not a forecast:
- If a well-implemented programme achieves the randomised-trial average (+0.15 SD in knowledge, +0.07 SD in behaviour) or the game-based result (+0.31 SD), Latvia's 18–24 knowledge scores should move measurably within one cohort.
- Behavioural effects (saving, avoiding expensive credit) will be smaller and slower. US evidence shows credit effects appearing **years** after the mandate (Urban et al. 2020).
- Realistic outcome measures: Latvijas Banka's 9th-grade index (12.3 / 20 in 2025) and the 18–24 band of the adult index.

## 3. What should be done, and how do we make an impact? (first draft)

**Goal:** make the compulsory financial literacy topic **real, measurable and engaging** by 2030.

**Proposed actions**

1. **A ready-to-teach financial literacy module** (about 10–12 lessons in grades 8–9 and again in grades 11–12), aligned with the EU/OECD youth competence framework.
   - Owners: the Ministry of Education and Science (IZM) and the National Centre for Education (VISC), with Latvijas Banka.
   - Content: interest and compound interest, inflation, risk and diversification, credit costs, fraud and money mules.
   - Why: the research shows that intensity matters (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020). The case for a dedicated block of lessons instead of scattered mentions comes from Cole et al. (2016) versus Urban et al. (2020).
2. **Teacher professional development.**
   - A short, certified course for social studies teachers, focused on the concepts teachers are weakest in (interest and compound interest).
   - Designed according to Compen et al. (2019).
   - Owners: Latvijas Banka, VISC and universities.
3. **Short, regular, gamified activities.**
   - Classroom games (an escape-room model, as in Cannistrà et al. 2024).
   - Competitions such as an Open Economics Olympiad.
   - **Finance professionals as volunteer "coaches"**, as in Lührmann et al. 2015.
   - Partners: Latvijas Banka, commercial banks (through Finance Latvia), and NGOs such as Jaunie Ekonomisti.
   - Spread through the year, not concentrated in one financial literacy week.
4. **A family component.** Take-home tasks that involve parents in a real household money decision. This targets the 79% of pupils who are not involved, and uses the parent spillovers found by Bruhn et al. and Frisancho.
5. **Measurement.**
   - Repeat Latvijas Banka's 9th-grade assessment every two years.
   - **Rejoin PISA financial literacy**, the next available cycle ⚠.
   - Set clear indicators in the post-2027 national strategy, fixing the weakness the strategy itself admits.

**Draft KPIs**
- 9th-grade index: 12.3 → 14 / 20 by 2030.
- 18–19-year-olds: 8.0 → 11 / 20 by 2030.
- Share of 9th graders who "never" learned about money at school: 36% → under 10%.
- Teachers passing the core-concepts assessment: baseline to be set → 80%.
- Latvia participating in the next PISA financial literacy cycle.

**Stakeholders**
- **Government:** IZM, VISC, the Saeima Education Committee.
- **Central bank:** Latvijas Banka, the strategy coordinator.
- **Commercial banks and industry:** Finance Latvia (volunteers, funding).
- **Schools:** teachers and their associations.
- **Parents.**
- **NGOs:** Jaunie Ekonomisti, Junior Achievement Latvia.
- **Media:** LSM.
- **European level:** the ECB and Commission (the EU Financial Literacy Strategy).

**Policy window**
- Latvia's national strategy ends in 2027.
- The EU Financial Literacy Strategy (2025) and the 2027 mid-term review of the Savings and Investments Union.
- The budget debate on learning materials (2026).

## Appendix A. Research methodology and conceptual frameworks

**Frameworks**
- **The OECD/INFE definition of financial literacy:** a combination of **knowledge, behaviour and attitudes**. This is also the structure of Latvijas Banka's 20-point index and of the OECD/INFE adult survey.
- **The EU/OECD financial competence frameworks** for children and youth, and for adults. These set learning outcomes by age and are the reference point of the EU Financial Literacy Strategy (2025).
- **Lusardi & Mitchell (2014, *Journal of Economic Literature*):** financial literacy as human capital, measured by the "Big Three" questions (interest, inflation, diversification), and linked to saving, investing and retirement planning.
- **Education production / implementation lens:** effects depend on **intensity** (Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020), **teacher quality** (Compen et al. 2019) and **timing** ("just-in-time" education, Fernandes et al. 2014).
- **Socialisation:** family and school as complementary channels (Grohmann et al. 2015).

**Methods of the key studies**

| Study | Method |
|---|---|
| Kaiser & Menkhoff (2020) | Meta-analysis of 37 (quasi-)experimental school studies; random-effects estimates; tests for publication bias |
| Kaiser et al. (2022) | Meta-analysis of 76 RCTs (>160,000 participants); multiple estimators; correction for publication bias |
| Fernandes et al. (2014) | Meta-analysis of 201 correlational and intervention studies |
| Bruhn et al. (2016) | Cluster-randomised controlled trial in 892 schools |
| Frisancho (2020) | Cluster-RCT in 300 schools, matched pairs, linked to credit-bureau records |
| Cannistrà et al. (2024) | Multi-country RCT, 2,220 students |
| Urban et al. (2020) | Difference-in-differences with neighbouring-state controls; credit-bureau panel |
| Cole et al. (2016) | Variation in state curriculum requirements; fixed-effects design |
| Mihno (2021, 2022) | Secondary analysis of Latvian PISA 2012 and 2018 data |
| Latvijas Banka surveys (2022, 2025) | Representative surveys using the OECD/INFE toolkit (adults n = 1,007; 9th graders n = 1,159) |
| Flash Eurobarometer 525 (2023) | EU-27 telephone and online survey; analysed in Demertzis et al. (2024) |

**Our own approach**
- Desk research, combining international peer-reviewed evidence with Latvian official data.
- Baltic comparison (Estonia as the benchmark).
- Mapping of causes to evidence-based solutions.
- Jaunie Ekonomisti's Olympiad data is used only as qualitative illustration, because it is non-representative.

**Limitations**
- Several Latvia figures come from secondary reporting (⚠).
- Most RCT evidence is from outside the EU or Baltics. Cannistrà et al. (2024), which includes Estonia, is the closest.
- Behavioural effects are small and slow to appear, so KPIs focus on knowledge in the short term.

## Appendix B. Jaunie Ekonomisti's own data (illustration only)

- An Open Economics Olympiad with about 200 participants from 30+ schools (2026).
- Participants' average score was 62%, with the hardest topics being **risk, return, inflation and compounding**. This matches Latvijas Banka's finding on teachers' weak knowledge of interest and compound interest.
- **Not representative** (self-selected, motivated students), so it is not used as evidence of the national level.

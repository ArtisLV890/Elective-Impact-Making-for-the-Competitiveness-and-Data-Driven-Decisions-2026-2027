# Part A. Does the deck answer all five questions?

| Required question | Where it is answered | Verdict | What to strengthen |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(i) What issue are you solving and why is it important?** | Slides 2–5 | **Fully covered** | Add the new risk data (gambling 29%, online loans +32%) to slide 5. End slide 5 with one sentence defining the issue precisely |
| **(ii) How does research and data support your intention?** | Slides 6–7 (plus 2–4 for data) | **Covered** | Make the link explicit: *each cause → the evidence → our step*. Add England (mandate fails without delivery) and "only ~9% of activities target teachers" to slide 7 |
| **(iii) What is your strategy, including interaction with media, policymakers and industry?** | Slides 8–10 | **Mostly covered** | The brief asks what steps **you** are taking. Make slides 9–10 first-person ("we will…"), and name concrete actions per group: a policy brief to IZM, an op-ed in LSM, a meeting with Finance Latvia |
| **(iv) What result do you expect (e.g. changing law or regulation)?** | Slide 11 | **Partly covered** | The question puts **law or regulation** first. Split slide 11 into **policy results** (regulations amended, strategy pillar, PISA participation, the seminar cycle accredited) and **learning results** (KPIs) |
| **(v) What regulatory changes should be applied?** | Slide 12 (plus appendix A5) | **Fully covered** | Add the quick win (accredit Latvijas Banka's teacher seminars under Regulation No. 569) and, optionally, the gambling-advertising rule |
| **Format: 10–15 slides, appendix for regulation, research and data** | 13 main slides + 6 appendix slides | **Meets the brief** | Appendix A5 (regulatory timeline) directly answers "changes in regulations" |

**Bottom line:** the deck answers all five questions. The two weak spots are **(iv)**, which should lead with the regulatory result, and **(iii)**, which should say more clearly what *we* do, not only what government should do. Both are small edits.

# Part B. Slide-by-slide guide

Each slide has one **headline** (the message, said in the first sentence), the **content**, **talking points**, the **visual**, the **source line**, a **transition** and a **time** guide (15-minute version).

## Slide 1. Title and hook (1:00)

- **Answers:** sets up (i).
- **Headline:** "Compulsory on paper, missing in practice".
- **Content:** title, subtitle ("Making financial education work for young people in Latvia"), team, course, date.
- **Talking points:**
  - *Hook option A (paradox):* "Financial literacy has been compulsory in every Latvian school for six years. Yet 36% of 9th graders say they have never learned about money at school."
  - *Hook option B (story):* "Imagine you're 15. A Telegram message offers €200 a week if you lend your bank account. Almost one in five Latvian 9th graders has received an offer like this."
  - One line on who you are: a youth NGO already running an economics Olympiad (about 200 students, 30+ schools).
- **Visual:** dark title slide.
- **Transition:** "Let's start with where Latvia stands."

## Slide 2. Latvia is joint last in the EU (1:00)

- **Answers:** (i), the size of the problem.
- **Headline:** "Latvia is joint last in the EU on financial literacy".
- **Content:** three stat cards:
  - **11%** high financial literacy (EU 18%, Estonia 23%);
  - **26%** no emergency savings (highest in the EU);
  - **27%** fraud victims in two years (74% faced attempts).
- **Talking points:**
  - "Only 11% of Latvian adults have high financial literacy, joint last with Portugal."
  - "The real gap is behaviour: we're 26th of 27. We have the thinnest financial buffers in the EU."
  - Optional: "Only 46% reach the OECD minimum score, against 58% in the OECD."
- **Visual:** stat cards (full EU ranking in appendix A1).
- **Source:** Flash Eurobarometer 525 (2023); OECD and Latvijas Banka (2026).
- **Transition:** "And it's worst among the young."

## Slide 3. The youngest adults are the least literate, and knowledge is falling (0:50)

- **Answers:** (i), who is affected.
- **Headline:** "The youngest adults are the least financially literate".
- **Content:** index by age (18–19: 8.0; 20–29: 11.0; all 12.2) and three key numbers: knowledge 5.1 → 3.9; 40% simple interest; 11% could cover a month's income.
- **Talking points:**
  - "Our central bank measures this. 18–19-year-olds score 8 out of 20, the lowest of any age group."
  - "And knowledge isn't improving, it's falling: compound interest correct answers dropped from 48% to 26% in seven years."
  - Be honest if asked: the 18–19 group is small, but 20–29 shows the same pattern.
- **Visual:** bar chart by age.
- **Source:** Latvijas Banka survey 2022 (n = 1,007).
- **Transition:** "So where should young people learn this? School. But…"

## Slide 4. School is where teenagers learn least (0:50)

- **Answers:** (i), the root of the problem.
- **Headline:** "School is where teenagers learn least about money".
- **Content:** sources chart (family 63%, internet 46%, school 9%; never at school 36%), plus three facts:
  - teachers' interest knowledge is insufficient;
  - there are no minimum lessons;
  - teaching is "superficial".
- **Talking points:**
  - "Teenagers learn about money from family and the internet. School comes last."
  - "That's not teachers' fault. Finance is one topic among many in social studies, with no guaranteed hours, and the central bank found teachers themselves are unsure about interest."
  - **Define the issue here, in one sentence:** "The problem we solve: compulsory financial education that isn't actually delivered."
- **Visual:** clustered bar plus fact cards.
- **Source:** Latvijas Banka 9th-grade assessment 2025 (n = 1,159); Latvijas Banka teacher survey 2024; LSM 2026.
- **Transition:** "And this gap is now dangerous."

## Slide 5. Why now: fraud and other risks (0:50)

- **Answers:** (i), why it is important *now*.
- **Headline:** "Why now: teenagers are already targets".
- **Content:**
  - 48% of 9th graders offered "easy money" online;
  - 18% asked to be money mules;
  - 31% think Bitcoin is legal tender;
  - €7.8m lost to fraud in H1 2026.
  - **Add:** 29% of 15–16-year-olds gambled for money; online loans +32% in 2025.
  - Policy-window box.
- **Talking points:**
  - "Three in four adults faced a scam attempt, one in four became a victim, and 37% told no one."
  - "Teenagers are recruited as money mules on Telegram. The penalty can be years in prison."
  - "The window is open: the national strategy ends in 2027, the OECD just proposed an action plan for Latvia, the government is drafting a fraud plan, and PISA 2025 was Latvia's worst ever."
- **Visual:** four stat tiles plus a dark policy-window box.
- **Source:** Latvijas Banka 2025; OECD 2026; Finance Latvia; ESPAD 2024; PTAC.
- **Transition:** "So what actually works?"

## Slide 6. Research: what works (1:30)

- **Answers:** (ii).
- **Headline:** "School financial education works when it is intensive".
- **Content:** effect-size chart (meta-analysis +0.33; Brazil +0.25; Peru +0.15; online game +0.31) and four lessons:
  1. intensity;
  2. teachers;
  3. real decisions;
  4. a mandate alone fails.
- **Talking points:**
  - "This is well researched: 76 randomised trials, over 160,000 people."
  - "Knowledge improves a lot; behaviour less and more slowly, so we must be realistic."
  - "The key warning: US states that made it compulsory on paper saw **no** effect. States with dedicated courses saw better credit scores. Latvia today is the first type."
  - "Scam education also works, but it fades within days or months unless it's repeated. One campaign is not enough."
- **Visual:** bar chart plus numbered lessons.
- **Source:** Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020; Kaiser et al. 2022; Bruhn et al. 2016; Frisancho 2020; Cannistrà et al. 2024; Cole et al. 2016; Urban et al. 2020; Burke et al. 2021.
- **Transition:** "Now compare that with what Latvia does today."

## Slide 7. Latvia: much activity, falling knowledge (1:00)

- **Answers:** (ii), why the current approach isn't enough.
- **Headline:** "Latvia has lots of activity, but knowledge is falling".
- **Content:** knowledge decline chart (2015 vs 2022). Activity facts:
  - 230+ activities;
  - Money Day in 78% of schools;
  - 28-partner memorandum;
  - **add:** only ~9% of activities target teachers.

  Benchmarks: **Estonia** (PISA 547, finance in teacher training) and **England** (compulsory since 2014, only 38% recall it, strengthening it now).
- **Talking points:**
  - "Latvia is busy: hundreds of events. But the strategy counts events, not learning, and knowledge fell."
  - "Only about one in ten activities targets teachers, the group research says matters most."
  - "England shows what happens with a paper mandate; Estonia shows what works."
- **Visual:** chart plus two benchmark boxes.
- **Source:** Latvijas Banka surveys and strategy evaluation 2025; OECD PISA 2018; APPG/MaPS (UK).
- **Transition:** "So here is what we propose."

## Slide 8. Our strategy: five steps (1:30)

- **Answers:** (iii).
- **Headline:** "Five steps from paper to practice".
- **Content:**
  1. **Module:** about 10–12 lessons across grades 7–9, plus secondary, each tied to a real decision (scams, money mules, crypto, finfluencers and gambling odds, online loans, the first salary and the pension).
  2. **Teacher training:** certify Latvijas Banka's new seminar cycle and count it in the required 36 hours.
  3. **Gamified practice:** the Olympiad, escape-room games, bank volunteers, spread through the year.
  4. **Family tasks.**
  5. **Measurement:** a 9th-grade survey every 2 years; PISA 2029.
- **Talking points:**
  - "Every step answers a research finding: intensity, teachers, real decisions, repetition, measurement."
  - "We don't create a new subject or institution. We use existing hours, existing training rules and existing partners."
  - "Step 3 is where our NGO already has proof: 200 students from 30 schools joined our Olympiad voluntarily."
- **Visual:** five numbered cards.
- **Transition:** "To make this happen we need four groups on board."

## Slide 9. Who we work with, and what *we* do (1:00)

- **Answers:** (iii), interaction with media, policymakers and industry (**required by the brief**).
- **Headline:** "Who we work with, and how".
- **Content:** four boxes, **written in first person**:
  - **Policymakers:** *we* send an evidence brief and draft amendments to IZM and VIAA, and request a Saeima Education Committee hearing.
  - **Central bank and industry:** *we* join Latvijas Banka's working group and ask Finance Latvia and banks to co-fund teacher training and send volunteers. Argument: EU payment rules make banks liable for weak fraud prevention.
  - **Schools and parents:** *we* recruit 10 pilot schools and design the family tasks.
  - **Media:** *we* publish op-eds with our data, pitch LSM and Delfi, and create scam-awareness content with student creators during Financial Literacy Week.
- **Talking points:** "Our role: bring evidence, the youth voice and a working format. We don't ask to run the system."
- **Visual:** 2×2 grid.
- **Transition:** "Here's the timeline."

## Slide 10. Roadmap 2026–2030 (1:00)

- **Answers:** (iii), the sequence of steps.
- **Headline:** "Roadmap 2026–2030".
- **Content:**
  - Dec 2026 – Feb 2027: evidence brief and meetings.
  - March 2027: launch at Financial Literacy Week.
  - Spring 2027: pilot in 10 schools.
  - Autumn 2027: proposals into the new strategy and regulations.
  - 2028–2030: rollout, PISA 2029.
- **Talking points:** "Quick wins first (evidence, media, pilot), then lock them into regulation when the new strategy is written in 2027."
- **Visual:** timeline.
- **Transition:** "What will this achieve?"

## Slide 11. Expected results (1:30)

- **Answers:** (iv). **Lead with law and regulation, as the brief asks.**
- **Headline:** "Expected results: new rules by 2028, better skills by 2030".
- **Content:** two columns.
  - **Policy results (by 2028):**
    - Regulations No. 747, 416 and 569 amended;
    - a school pillar with youth targets in the 2028+ strategy;
    - Latvijas Banka's teacher seminars accredited;
    - Latvia in PISA 2029.
  - **Learning results (by 2030):**
    - 9th-grade index 12.3 → 14.0;
    - simple interest 57% → 75%;
    - "never at school" 36% → under 10%;
    - 80% of teachers trained.
  - Cost box: €0.2–0.35m set-up plus €0.1–0.2m a year, about 1% of a year's fraud losses.
- **Talking points:**
  - "The first result is institutional: the rules change, so it doesn't depend on one minister or one project."
  - "Then measurable learning gains, within the range the research shows is realistic."
  - "Behaviour, like saving and avoiding scams, follows more slowly, so we track it over years."
- **Visual:** two-column KPI layout plus cost card. *(This changes the current slide 11.)*
- **Transition:** "Concretely, which rules must change?"

## Slide 12. Regulatory changes (2:00)

- **Answers:** (v).
- **Headline:** "Four regulatory changes that make it stick".
- **Content:** table with instrument, change and owner:
  1. **Education standards** (Cabinet Regulations No. 747 and 416): explicit, assessed outcomes (compound interest, inflation, risk, digital fraud, money mules) plus a minimum block of about 10 lessons.
  2. **Teacher training rules** (No. 569, 36 hours every 3 years): a required financial and digital literacy course for social-studies teachers, within the 36 hours. Precedent: since 2025, PE teachers who teach swimming must take a 24-hour course.
  3. **National strategy 2028+:** a school pillar with youth outcome targets, linked to the OECD action plan and the fraud-prevention plan.
  4. **PISA 2029:** the financial literacy option.

  **Add as quick wins:** accredit Latvijas Banka's seminars now; tighter youth-targeted gambling advertising.

  **EU tailwind:** Commission strategy (2025); European Parliament resolution (April 2026, 502 votes for); EU payment rules.
- **Talking points:**
  - "None of this needs a new law. They are Cabinet regulations and strategy decisions, achievable in one policy cycle."
  - "There is a precedent: since 2025, PE teachers who teach swimming must take a 24-hour course. We ask for the same for social-studies teachers and money."
  - "And Europe is pushing in the same direction: the Parliament voted 502 to 46 for financial education in school curricula."
- **Visual:** table (detail in appendix A5).
- **Transition:** "So, our three asks."

## Slide 13. Close: three asks (1:00)

- **Answers:** summarises (iii)–(v).
- **Headline:** "Make compulsory financial education real".
- **Content:**
  - **IZM and VIAA:** outcomes, minimum lessons, teacher training in regulation.
  - **Latvijas Banka:** a school pillar with youth targets.
  - **Banks and Finance Latvia:** co-fund training, volunteer coaches.
- **Closing line:** "We already made financial literacy compulsory. Now let's make it real, before the scammers teach our teenagers first."

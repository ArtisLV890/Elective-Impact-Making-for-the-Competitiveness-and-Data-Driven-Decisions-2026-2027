# Appendix: Research methodology and conceptual frameworks

## Conceptual framework

**Proximity framework (Boschma, 2005).** Boschma's paper is a conceptual, critical literature review in economic geography. It distinguishes five dimensions of proximity that enable interactive learning between organisations:

- **Cognitive:** a shared knowledge base;
- **Organisational:** being part of the same arrangement or network;
- **Social:** trust-based personal relations;
- **Institutional:** shared rules, norms and incentives;
- **Geographical:** physical distance.

Its central argument is that geographical proximity is **neither necessary nor sufficient** for learning and innovation. It works only when combined with the other dimensions, and too much proximity can also cause lock-in. We use the framework as a diagnostic lens. The Knowledge Mile has geographical proximity (five universities within about 3 km) but lacks organisational proximity (no joint body or agreement), institutional proximity (separate rules, no shared course catalogue) and social proximity (students and researchers rarely interact across campuses).

**Innovation districts and the "city as organiser".** The comparison table applies the innovation-district view (Katz & Wagner, 2014): a district needs economic, physical and networking assets plus a governance body. We compare cities on one variable, **the city's role in governing its knowledge district**. That role ranges from co-founder (Tartu, Vilnius) and co-funder (Tallinn, Eindhoven) to signatory only (Riga).

## Methodology of the key studies we draw on

**Boudreau et al. (2017), randomised field experiment.**

- **Design:** scientists at Harvard Medical School who attended a symposium tied to a new grant programme were **randomly assigned** to 90-minute structured information-sharing breakout sessions. Because the assignment was random, pairs of scientists who shared a session differed from other pairs only in having met.
- **Outcome:** whether a pair later **co-applied** for a grant.
- **Analysis:** pair-level comparison of treated pairs (same session) against control pairs.
- **Result:** the session raised the co-application probability by 75%, from 0.16% to 0.28%, mostly among pairs in the same field.
- **Strength:** randomisation gives a causal estimate of what reducing "search costs" does.
- **Limitation:** one elite institution and one type of outcome.

**Atkin, Chen & Popov (2022), observational study using smartphone location data.**

- **Design:** the authors measure **face-to-face meetings** between workers of different technology firms in Silicon Valley from anonymised smartphone location pings at firm sites. They link these meetings to **patent citations** between the same firms.
- **Identification:** they use an instrumental-variables strategy to separate the effect of meeting from firms' tendency to meet when they already share knowledge.
- **Result:** a 25% reduction in meetings implies about an 8% drop in citations.
- **Status:** an NBER working paper, not yet peer reviewed.

**Catalini (2018), natural experiment** (if cited in the text).

- **Design:** asbestos removal at the Jussieu campus in Paris forced labs to relocate largely for reasons unrelated to research. This created quasi-random variation in which labs ended up next to each other.
- **Result:** co-location made two labs **3.5 times** more likely to collaborate.

**LaSER (2026), qualitative policy study of the Riga Knowledge Mile.** The study combines:

- desk research and administrative data (Rīgas satiksme ticket validations, Ministry of Education student residence data);
- 16 in-depth interviews on research commercialisation;
- interviews with LU, RSU, RTU, RISEBA and Riga City Council deputies (summer 2026);
- student focus groups with **mental mapping**;
- GIS mapping of services and land ownership.

It is descriptive and has no causal analysis. We use it for the local diagnosis: the memorandum's lack of an owner, KPIs and budget, and the missing interaction between campuses.

**Krasnopjorovs (2022, Latvijas Banka).** A descriptive comparison of Riga with other Baltic and European metropolitan areas on income and quality-of-life indicators. It is the source of the estimate that Riga lags Vilnius and Tallinn by 7–8 years.

## Our own approach

1. **Secondary data analysis.** We downloaded the data directly on 1 November 2026:
   - **Eurostat:** GDP per inhabitant in PPS for NUTS 3 regions (`nama_10r_3gdp`; Riga = LV00A, Vilnius = LT011, Tallinn = EE001), R&D intensity (`rd_e_gerdtot`) and tertiary attainment of 25–34-year-olds (`edat_lfse_03`);
   - **Statistics Latvia:** population (IRS031) and mobile students (IGA050);
   - **Official sites:** the European Innovation Scoreboard 2026 country profiles and the QS Best Student Cities 2027 ranks.
2. **Comparative case analysis.** We built a structured comparison of how seven cities govern their knowledge districts, using the city's role as the comparison variable. It draws on official city, agency and park websites.
3. **Legal and document review.** We read the consolidated texts of Cabinet Regulations No. 240 and 793 on likumi.lv, the Ministry of Education's public answer on recognising courses taken elsewhere, the University of Latvia's recognition procedure, and the 2023–2024 Riga memoranda. The aim was to establish whether cross-university electives are legally restricted. They are not: MK No. 240 sets no rule for the free-choice part.
4. **Illustrative estimates (not forecasts).**
   - **Collaboration:** 10% of the Mile's ~4,000 researchers is 400 people, or about 80,000 possible pairs. Applying Boudreau et al.'s uplift of 0.12 percentage points per pair gives **about 95 additional collaborating pairs**.
   - **Spending:** 1,000 students × LaSER's €15,780 annual spending per international student gives **≈ €15m a year**.

## Limitations

- **Missing data:** Riga's 2024 regional GDP is not yet published. No official stay rate for international graduates exists in Latvia; RSU's 5% is the university's own figure.
- **Transferability:** the causal studies come from the US and France, so their effect sizes may not transfer to Riga.
- **No Baltic evaluation:** no causal evaluation of a Baltic innovation district exists, so the comparison shows **association, not causation**.
- **Secondary sources:** venture-capital and per-university student figures come from secondary sources (Dealroom, Go Vilnius, LaSER).
